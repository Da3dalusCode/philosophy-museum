## Sources consulted

### Plato primary corpus and editions

- Plato’s dialogues were consulted as a corpus through *Plato: Complete Works*, edited by John M. Cooper with D. S. Hutchinson (Hackett, 1997). The edition identifies individual translators, includes works transmitted under Plato’s name, and separates questions of authenticity from the convenient idea of a uniform authorial textbook.
- The Perseus catalog and its Paul Shorey *Republic* supplied a public Greek-and-English text with stable work and edition metadata. The existing Plato Museum panels’ Stephanus-numbered Perseus links were checked as primary-text access points.
- The article relies chiefly on *Apology*, *Crito*, *Meno*, *Gorgias*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*, *Parmenides*, *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Timaeus*, *Philebus*, and *Laws*. The disputed *Seventh Letter* is discussed only as contested evidence, not as an unquestioned autobiography.
- Primary works were paraphrased. No decorative exact quotation, translation-dependent slogan, or fabricated page reference was added.

### Plato specialist references and scholarship

- The current Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Plato was used for dialogue form, speaker attribution, corpus problems, chronology, and the limits of reconstructing Plato’s own voice.
- SEP entries on Plato’s middle-period metaphysics and epistemology, rhetoric and poetry, myths, and the *Timaeus* were consulted for Forms, participation, recollection, hypothesis, art, persuasion, mythic genres, cosmology, teleology, receptacle, and necessity.
- The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the *Republic* was used for the work’s argument about justice and happiness, with the existing Museum panel’s dedicated SEP ethics-and-politics reference retained.
- David Ebrey and Richard Kraut’s edited *Cambridge Companion to Plato*, second edition (Cambridge University Press, 2022), was used through accessible introductory, contents, chronology, and bibliographic materials to map the specialist range across context, stylometry, Socrates, inquiry, love, ethics, politics, metaphysics, aesthetics, religion, mathematics, and psychology. Inaccessible chapters were not represented as fully read.

### Platonism, the Academy, and late antiquity

- Charles Brittain’s “Plato and Platonism” in *The Oxford Handbook of Plato* supplied a specialist overview of the delayed formation of a more systematic Platonist movement and the historical difference between Plato and later Platonism. Only accessible abstract and bibliographic material was used where the full chapter was unavailable.
- SEP entries on Arcesilaus, Carneades, Philo of Larissa, and ancient skepticism were consulted for the skeptical Academy, dialectical method, criteria of truth, persuasive appearances, and the difficulty of attributing positive doctrines to fragmentarily known figures.
- The IEP entry on Middle Platonism was used for Antiochus, the return to systematic dialogue interpretation, divine intellect, and the diversity of the period.
- SEP entries on Plotinus and Neoplatonism were consulted for the One, Intellect, Soul, procession, return, virtue, embodiment, evil, and the differences among Plotinus, Iamblichus, Proclus, and other late-antique Platonists.
- Oxford and Cambridge university-press records for John Dillon’s *The Middle Platonists*, John Glucker’s *Antiochus and the Late Academy*, Harold Tarrant’s *Scepticism or Platonism?*, and John Dillon’s *The Heirs of Plato* were used to identify the scholarly field and its disputes. Unread or inaccessible chapters were not used as evidence for specific prose.

### Religious, cross-cultural, Renaissance, and modern receptions

- SEP’s “Greek Sources in Arabic and Islamic Philosophy” was used for the translation movement, al-Kindi’s circle, and the already intertwined Aristotelian and Neoplatonic materials transmitted in Arabic, including the *Theology of Aristotle*.
- SEP entries on Solomon Ibn Gabirol and Isaac Israeli, together with SEP’s account of Arabic and Islamic influence on Judaic thought, were consulted for Jewish Neoplatonisms, divine Will, universal hylomorphism, negative theology, return, and the Arabic-Hebrew-Latin crossings that make a one-direction European story untenable.
- SEP entries on Marsilio Ficino and the Cambridge Platonists were used for Renaissance translation and commentary, the *Platonic Theology*, perennial philosophy, and the later-named seventeenth-century group’s disagreements and encounters with Cartesianism, mechanism, experimental science, and religious controversy.
- SEP entries on Platonism in metaphysics and in the philosophy of mathematics were consulted to distinguish historical capital-P Platonism from contemporary lower-case platonisms about abstract objects, mathematics, properties, and propositions.

Search-result snippets, generic summaries, quotation sites, videos, and AI-generated material were not used as evidence or prose sources. Wikipedia was removed from the two visitor bibliographies and was not used as an evidentiary source. Publisher descriptions, tables of contents, and abstracts were used only to identify editions, contributors, and the shape of specialist disputes; inaccessible arguments were not paraphrased as if fully consulted.

## Editorial decisions and major corrections

Plato is presented as a dramatic, indirect, and potentially developing writer. “Dialogue form” is not treated as one unchanging device: refutational exchanges, long speeches, myths, classifications, and late technical conversations do different work. The familiar early-middle-late sequence remains a useful hypothesis, especially around a stylometrically related late group, but not a license to date every dialogue confidently or resolve every tension as a change of mind. Authenticity questions remain visible, particularly for the Letters.

No statement by Plato’s Socrates is automatically attributed to either the historical Socrates or Plato. The article distinguishes the historical reconstruction problem, the dramatic speaker, and a possible authorial commitment. It also gives the Eleatic Stranger, Timaeus, and the Athenian visitor their proper importance. Myths and irony are treated as philosophical forms whose local purpose must be interpreted, not as either literal doctrine or decorative escape from argument.

Forms are presented as a family of explanatory proposals and problems across *Phaedo*, *Republic*, *Parmenides*, *Sophist*, and related dialogues. The article does not describe a simple second physical world. Recollection is not made into effortless innate omniscience; mathematical education is both exemplary and criticized for taking hypotheses and diagrams for granted; *Theaetetus* is allowed to end without a final definition of knowledge.

Psychology and ethics now connect Socratic intellectualism, *Gorgias*, the divided soul, pleasure in *Philebus*, and educable erōs. Love is not sanitized into disembodied admiration. Rhetoric and poetry are handled as internally divided problems: *Gorgias* criticizes manipulation, *Phaedrus* imagines a truth-directed art of persuasion, and *Republic*’s real program of cultural regulation is held beside Plato’s own literary practice.

Natural philosophy now receives a dedicated account of the *Timaeus*: craftsman, model, receptacle, necessity, mathematical order, teleology, embodiment, and the limited status of a likely account. This material is not credited with modern science and its ancient biological hierarchy is not ignored.

The political treatment neither acquits nor convicts Plato through one slogan. Philosopher-rulers, censorship, civic falsehood, hierarchy, women guardians, managed reproduction, communal child-rearing, law, and coercion are addressed directly. The women-guardian argument is recognized as revisionary within its setting but not expanded into general emancipation. “Eugenic” is used cautiously as an illuminating modern comparison rather than a claim that Plato participated in modern racial science. The *Statesman* and *Laws* show continuing revision without yielding a simple liberal or democratic Plato.

Platonism is presented as a long reception tradition whose disagreements begin in the Old Academy. Speusippus and Xenocrates are not placeholders on a road to Plotinus, and Academic skepticism is not an interruption from outside Platonism. Middle Platonists, Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus, and Proclus develop distinct answers about Forms, intellect, soul, contemplation, theurgy, participation, procession, and return. Neoplatonism is a useful modern label for major late-antique developments, while its members ordinarily claimed to interpret Plato rather than found a novelty.

Plato, Platonism, and Neoplatonism remain distinct throughout. Plato is the dialogue author whose commitments require reconstruction. Platonism is the diverse history of attempts to inherit him. Neoplatonism names a late-antique family that systematizes hierarchy and return in ways no single Platonic dialogue states. Contemporary lower-case platonism about abstract objects is narrower again and does not imply acceptance of ancient soul, politics, religion, or cosmology.

Religious reception is not narrated as a Christian annexation of a European tradition. Christian, Jewish, and Islamic thinkers transform Platonist materials through creation, incarnation, grace, scripture, commandment, law, prophecy, divine Will, and freedom. Syriac and Arabic translation, Jewish philosophy written in Arabic, Hebrew and Latin crossings, Byzantine copying, and late-antique pseudo-Aristotelian texts make the tradition multilingual and multidirectional. Ibn Gabirol, Isaac Israeli, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Suhrawardi appear as philosophers with their own projects rather than passive transmitters.

## Source limitations and later claim review

This batch is source-informed depth work, not claim review. Accessible encyclopedia entries, primary-text portals, publisher metadata, tables of contents, abstracts, and bibliographies do not substitute for a complete specialist monograph library. The Old Academy is known through fragmentary and interested reports; Arcesilaus and Carneades wrote no surviving works; Plato’s chronology, Letters, unwritten teachings, Sicilian biography, and authorial positions remain disputed. The article retains those limits rather than filling them with certainty.

A later claim review should map durable Stephanus locators for each dialogue claim; distinguish translation choices; review the corpus and chronology section against current stylometric scholarship; and give special attention to Forms, recollection, mathematical education, the *Timaeus*, women guardians, managed reproduction, law, and every claim about Plato’s biography. Platonism claim review should separately map the Old Academy, skeptical Academy, Middle Platonism, each major late-antique thinker, the *Theology of Aristotle*, Syriac and Arabic transmission, Jewish Neoplatonisms, Islamic transformations, Ficino, the Cambridge Platonists, and contemporary metaphysical and mathematical uses. Until that work is completed, both records properly remain bibliography-only.
