## Final structure and editorial decisions

The replacement article uses eighteen ordered sections:

1. A philosopher of changing problems
2. Philology, illness, and a life outside the academy
3. Tragedy, Schopenhauer, and the Wagnerian hope
4. Free spirits and the turn toward historical psychology
5. Truth, interpretation, and perspectivism
6. Embodiment, drives, affects, and the divided self
7. Genealogy as history, interpretation, and critique
8. Master and slave moralities, nobility, and ressentiment
9. Debt, punishment, bad conscience, and guilt
10. Ascetic ideals and the critique of Christianity
11. The death of God and the crisis of nihilism
12. Will to power without a single settled doctrine
13. Eternal recurrence as cosmology, test, and literary event
14. The Übermensch and self-overcoming
15. Hierarchy, democracy, nationalism, and antisemitism
16. Published books, notebooks, and *The Will to Power*
17. Influence, fascist appropriation, and competing inheritances
18. Live disputes and a responsible reading path

The familiar early/middle/later periodization is used as a reading aid while its borders and continuities remain disputed. Philology is treated as both a historical profession and a source of later attention to transmission, language, interpretation, and editorial authority. The break with Schopenhauer and Wagner is a transformation of inherited questions, not a single conversion scene.

Perspectivism is presented as a critique of view-from-nowhere objectivity and a positive discipline of comparing affective interpretations. It is not relativism, and disagreement remains over truth, representation, psychology, and ontology. Naturalism is likewise left open among methodological, psychological, biological, and metaphysical readings.

Genealogy does not refute a value merely by exposing a contingent history. Its critical force may depend on false self-understanding, present function, flourishing, power, honesty, or affirmation. Master/slave morality is not a timeless caste chart or a wholesale recommendation of master morality. Ressentiment identifies a proposed reactive pattern, not every protest by subordinated people. Bad conscience, guilt, punishment, promising, and the ascetic ideal are treated as ambivalent formations that can deepen capacities while intensifying self-cruelty.

The death of God is a cultural event involving the afterlife of Christian morality, not only personal atheism. Nihilism is a crisis of valuation, and notebook taxonomies are not silently promoted to published doctrine. Revaluation is embodied transformation rather than arbitrary preference. Competing perfectionist, naturalist, constitutivist, aesthetic, and skeptical readings of positive ethics remain visible.

Will to power is not reduced to domination or asserted as one universal metaphysical substance. Its psychological, evaluative, biological, and cosmological scopes remain disputed, with an explicit warning where systematic readings depend heavily on notebooks. Eternal recurrence remains open among cosmological, existential, ethical, identity, and literary interpretations. The Übermensch is not a racial master species, but the article does not detach it from hierarchy, cultivation, breeding rhetoric, or political difficulty. Self-overcoming occurs within embodied inheritance rather than through limitless self-invention.

Nietzsche’s attacks on a self-causing will are held beside his praise of achieved self-command, promising, and responsibility for oneself. Politics is not settled by calling him fascist, liberal, anarchist, apolitical, or a direct precursor of a present ideology. His rejection of German nationalism and contemporary antisemitic movements is explicit, as are his anti-egalitarianism, hostility to democracy and socialism, hierarchy, misogyny, racialized language, and selective cultivation. Anti-antisemitism is not used as proof that every treatment of Jews and Judaism is free of prejudice.

The article distinguishes completed public works, completed works published after the 1889 collapse, and the Nachlass. *The Will to Power* is described as an unauthorized selection and arrangement of notebook fragments, not a completed book. Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, Heinrich Köselitz/Peter Gast, the 1901 and 1906 editions, archive control, and the Colli–Montinari restoration of chronological relationships are named. “Forgery” is treated cautiously because the central problems are selection, ordering, transcription, and framing rather than the wholesale invention of Nietzschean sentences.

## Rejected material and source limitations

- Wikipedia was removed from the visitor bibliography and used neither as evidence nor as a source of prose. Search-result snippets, generic quotation sites, social-media threads, videos, motivational summaries, and AI-generated material were rejected.
- Paywalled or preview-limited chapters were used only at the level of accessible abstracts, tables of contents, and publisher descriptions. Their inaccessible arguments were not paraphrased as if fully consulted.
- *The Will to Power* was rejected as an authored primary book. Notebook material is treated through chronologically responsible critical editions and is not assigned the same authority as published work.
- No isolated quotation was used to settle Nietzsche’s politics, truth theory, recurrence, or agency. No exact page number, medical diagnosis, image claim, source credit, or license was invented.
- The older standalone SEP life/works and moral/political entries were consulted as archived scholarship but not substituted for the substantially revised 2026 SEP synthesis or presented as current consensus.
