## Sources consulted

### Kant primary texts and editions

- Kant’s *Critique of Pure Reason*, especially both prefaces and introductions, the Transcendental Aesthetic, the Analytic of Concepts and Principles, the Paralogisms, and the Doctrine of Method, supplied the architecture for the new method and apperception sections. The [Cambridge Companion to Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-kants-critique-of-pure-reason/B317B04E22B2BB20871F583CD7C24BA1) was used to check the argument map and later reception.
- The *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* was consulted for Kant’s retrospective presentation of transcendental inquiry and his response to the first Critique’s reception.
- The *Metaphysics of Morals*, especially the distinction between right and virtue and the discussions of property, public law, punishment, and political authority, anchors the new legal-philosophy section.
- *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* and the [Cambridge edition introduction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/kant-metaphysical-foundations-of-natural-science/introduction/417199F5B9D09F63C4AB4E10BABC0E8A) were consulted for motion, matter, mathematics, and the relation between transcendental principles and empirical physics.
- The [UC San Diego Kant texts portal](https://philosophyfaculty.ucsd.edu/faculty/ctolley/texts/kant.html) was used as a public primary-text access point. No translation-specific phrase, long quotation, or fabricated page number was added.

### Kantianism, German Idealism, and nineteenth-century reception

- [SEP: Immanuel Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/) supplied the chronology of Reinhold, Fichte, and Kant’s repudiation of Fichtean identification with the critical philosophy.
- SEP entries on [Hegel](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel/), [Schopenhauer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schopenhauer/), and [Phenomenology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/phenomenology/) were used to distinguish influence, reconstruction, and criticism rather than narrating one continuous school.
- The [Cambridge Companion to Kant and Modern Philosophy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-kant-and-modern-philosophy/0648DD16F3F3225B8F22FC9C920CEEF9) and the [Cambridge Companion to Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-kants-critique-of-pure-reason/B317B04E22B2BB20871F583CD7C24BA1) were used to map the first Critique’s early, German Idealist, neo-Kantian, continental, and analytic receptions.
- Frederick Beiser’s *The Genesis of Neo-Kantianism, 1796–1880* and Sebastian Luft’s *The Neo-Kantian Reader* supplied the reading-path anchors for the plurality between German Idealism and the classical neo-Kantian schools.

- The archived [SEP entry on Neo-Kantianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2021/entries/neo-kantianism/) was the principal reference for the Marburg and Southwest schools, anti-psychologism, transcendental method, scientific objectivity, historical science, and the schools’ internal differences.

- SEP entries on [Rawls](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/) and [Habermas](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/) were used to map political constructivism, public reason, discourse ethics, democratic legitimacy, and their critics.

2. Synthesis, imagination, apperception, and the unity of a knower
3. Right, coercion, property, and the limits of ethical virtue

## Editorial decisions and direct contradiction reconciliation

Kantianism is presented as reception history, not Kant’s philosophy under a second route. The article begins with the difference between historically reconstructing Kant and using a Kantian argument. Each major movement is described through what it retains, transforms, and rejects. German Idealism is therefore both a foundational reception and a departure from Kant’s limits. Neo-Kantianism is treated in its historically specific Marburg and Southwest forms before broader uses of “neo-Kantian” are introduced.

The article does not turn twentieth-century philosophy into a triumphal family tree. Analytic and continental inheritances are presented as internally plural and as emerging from shared disputes, institutions, exile, and professional reclassification. Strawson’s selective reconstruction is not equated with Sellars or McDowell; Husserlian transcendental phenomenology is not equated with Heidegger; critical theory is not reduced to applied categorical imperatives.

Practical Kantianism is equally plural. Rawlsian political constructivism, Korsgaardian accounts of agency, O’Neill’s principles of shared action, Herman’s moral judgment, Wood’s value-centered reading, and Habermasian discourse ethics are related without being collapsed. Constructivism is one disputed inheritance rather than the canonical definition of Kantian ethics.

Criticism is part of the reception rather than a final addendum. Marxian, Nietzschean, feminist, critical-race, disability, care-ethical, and decolonial challenges test abstraction, embodiment, dependency, material power, and excluded standing. Kant’s universal language is neither treated as an automatic acquittal nor declared philosophically irrelevant without argument.

## Source limitations and later claim review

This batch is a sourced editorial synthesis, not a completed claim review. Some specialist books and chapters were available only through publisher metadata, abstracts, or limited previews; the article does not attribute their inaccessible arguments. The history necessarily selects from a larger field, especially in international neo-Kantianism, philosophy of science, phenomenology, critical theory, and practical philosophy.

A later claim review should map primary locators for the A/B edition changes; synthesis, imagination, schematism, and apperception; the distinction between right and virtue; possession, punishment, and resistance; the natural-science architecture; and each claim about scientific change. Kant’s race, gender, anthropology, colonialism, and historical-development claims require their own high-risk locator pass.

For Kantianism, later mapping should distinguish Reinhold, Jacobi, Maimon, and Schulze; Fichte’s successive Wissenschaftslehre presentations; Schelling’s periods; Hegel’s criticism of Kant; Schopenhauer and the non-Hegelian nineteenth century; the institutional and doctrinal boundaries of Marburg and Southwest neo-Kantianism; international receptions; analytic and continental reconstructions; ethical and political constructivisms; and critical scholarship on exclusion. Until that work is completed, both pages properly remain bibliography-only.
