## Editorial decisions and major corrections

Hume is presented as a system-building investigator of human nature, not merely the author of a skeptical puzzle about induction. Epistemology, passions, ethics, politics, history, political economy, religion, and aesthetics are treated as connected applications of an explanatory method. The copy principle is qualified as applying normally to simple ideas rather than asserted as an exceptionless slogan. Belief is distinguished from freely chosen assent, and custom is an explanation of unavoidable inference rather than a deductive justification of induction.

The two definitions of cause are left open among regularity, projectivist, and skeptical-realist interpretations. Hume is not made to deny events, causal reasoning, or every possible power; the article distinguishes what experience presents, what the mind contributes, and what Hume may leave unknowable. The bundle account is not presented as a finished solution: the *Treatise* Appendix’s unresolved difficulty about the unity of perceptions is central. “Reason is the slave of the passions” is not converted into praise of impulse; reason’s indispensable roles in discovering relations, anticipating consequences, and correcting belief remain visible.

The religion sections separate criticism of miracles, design, divine attributes, and providence from the historical psychology of religious belief. Philo is not automatically identified with Hume at every point, and the *Dialogues*’ dramatic form remains interpretively consequential. Politics is neither modern democratic liberalism nor a simple apology for obedience. History and economics receive substantive treatment because Hume’s public reputation and explanatory ambitions extended far beyond the works now assigned to epistemology courses.

Hume’s racist revision to “Of National Characters” is named plainly. It is not treated as an irrelevant personal blemish or excused by period context. The article explains the inconsistency between a hierarchy defended through selective counterexample and Hume’s own ideals of corrected comparison, attention to contrary cases, and suspicion of prejudice. It does not claim that one paragraph alone settles every question about race, empire, commerce, or the rest of the corpus.

Empiricism is defined as a contested family of genetic, justificatory, methodological, and explanatory commitments. It is not “all knowledge comes from sensory experience,” passive data collection, anti-theory, or the belief that observation is neutral. Locke, Berkeley, and Hume share important pressures but are not represented as members of a self-declared British party. The rationalist/empiricist division is retained as a comparison tool while its post-Kantian and nineteenth-century construction is explicit.

Ancient medical and skeptical “empiric” practices, Aristotelian appeals to perception, and medieval work on cognition are treated as precedents and neighboring debates, not as a single continuous school. Experimental philosophy foregrounds instruments, testimony, replication, histories, and collective discipline. Kant transforms rather than averages the inherited problem. Logical empiricism is presented as a plural movement with semantic, scientific, and political ambitions, not a crude verification slogan; Quinean naturalism, constructive empiricism, theory-ladenness, underdetermination, and contemporary model-based science appear as later revisions and disputes.

Comparisons with Indian pramāṇa debates and Chinese accounts of knowing are deliberately local and question-based. They identify perception, inference, testimony, skill, and correction without claiming that Nyāya, Buddhist epistemology, Mohism, or Confucian inquiry are versions of British empiricism. The article warns that applying a European retrospective category globally can erase each tradition’s own vocabulary, aims, genres, and institutions.

## Source limitations and later claim review

Publisher descriptions, tables of contents, and accessible abstracts were used only to map specialist debates when full chapters were unavailable. The Oxford race chapter in particular remains a priority for full-text claim review. No inaccessible argument is paraphrased as if completely consulted. The new article offers an editorially responsible synthesis, not a definitive solution to disputes over causal realism, the unity of consciousness, moral realism, religion, political moderation, the significance of Hume’s histories, or the reach of his racial hierarchy.

A later claim review should map durable primary locators for the copy principle and its exceptions; belief and association; the two definitions of cause; induction; the *Treatise* Appendix; passion, liberty, and responsibility; sympathy and general points of view; justice and allegiance; miracles and natural religion; taste; political essays; monetary and trade mechanisms; the *History*; and every statement about race. Empiricism claim review should separately map the historiography of the category, the experimental/speculative distinction, ancient and medieval precedents, each early-modern figure, Kant, the Vienna Circle, Quine, naturalism, theory-ladenness, constructive empiricism, and the carefully bounded cross-cultural comparisons. Until that work is completed, both records properly remain bibliography-only.
