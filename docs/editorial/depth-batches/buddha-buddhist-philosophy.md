## Research method and sources consulted

### Early Buddhist textual materials

- SN 56.11, the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu’s translation, was consulted for the Middle Way, eightfold path, four truths, and the distinct task attached to each truth.
- SN 22.59, the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*, in Ñāṇamoli Thera’s translation, was consulted for the aggregate-based analysis of not-self, impermanence, vulnerability, and lack of command.
- MN 26, the *Ariyapariyesanā Sutta*, was consulted for the noble-search narrative, teachers, ascetic search, awakening, and decision to teach.
- MN 63, the *Cūḷamāluṅkyovāda Sutta*, was consulted for undeclared questions, the poisoned-arrow simile, and the practical purpose of philosophical restraint.
- DN 16, the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta*, was consulted for final-days traditions, authority after the Buddha, discipline, death, funeral, and relic memory.
- SuttaCentral was retained as a portal for early discourses and parallels. Individual pages that did not expose readable text to the research tooling were not represented as fully consulted.
- 84000 was retained as a primary-text translation portal for later Tibetan-canonical materials, not as evidence that every linked translation was read for this batch.

### Historical and specialist references

- The current Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the Buddha and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the Buddha were consulted for disputed chronology, oral transmission, early-text comparison, historical reconstruction, the four truths as practices, dependent arising, no-self, karma, rebirth, reason, and modern misreadings.
- SEP entries on Abhidharma, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, mind in Indian Buddhist philosophy, ethics in Indian Buddhism, and Dharmakīrti were consulted for post-canonical system building, dharma analysis, time and momentariness, emptiness, two truths, cognition, idealism disputes, pramāṇa, language, and ethical theory.
- SEP entries on Tiantai, Huayan, Chan, Japanese Zen, Japanese Pure Land philosophy, Korean Buddhism, and Tibetan two-truths traditions were consulted to prevent an India-only account and to distinguish regional philosophical developments.
- Peter Harvey’s *An Introduction to Buddhism* (Cambridge University Press) was used through accessible publisher overview and contents material as a broad historical and pedagogical reference.
- Philip C. Almond’s *The Buddha* (Cambridge University Press) was used through accessible publisher description and bibliographic material for the layered historical and received figure. Inaccessible chapters were not treated as read.
- Mark Siderits and Jan Westerhoff’s edited *Studies in Buddhist Philosophy* (Oxford University Press) was used through accessible abstract, contents, and bibliographic framing to map specialist problems; inaccessible essays were not paraphrased as though fully consulted.
- David L. McMahan’s *The Making of Buddhist Modernism* (Oxford University Press) was consulted through its accessible detailed abstract and publisher metadata for modernist selection, demythologization, psychology, science, and globalization.

## Editorial decisions and corrections

The Buddha is presented as a historically difficult teacher probably active around the fifth century BCE, not as a securely dated modern author. The article distinguishes comparative early-text reconstruction, communal transmission, later biography, later Buddhist system building, and modern reception. Developed Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, pramāṇa, Buddha-nature, Pure Land, Chan, and tantric theories are not projected backward into the historical Buddha.

The four truths are explained as interlocking tasks rather than a pessimistic creed. Dukkha is broader than constant misery; craving is not every desire; dependent arising is not the vague slogan that everything is connected; karma is intentional action rather than fate or victim-blaming; not-self does not erase conventional persons or responsibility; and mindfulness is one capacity within an ethical, contemplative, and intellectual path.

Buddhist Philosophy is presented as a multilingual, transregional, internally contested family rather than a single timeless system. Abhidharma schools, Mahāyāna movements, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, pramāṇa, Theravāda, Tiantai, Huayan, Chan/Zen, Pure Land, Korean, Japanese, and Tibetan formations are given distinct problems and institutions. Empty means lacking intrinsic nature, not nonexistent. Yogācāra is not reduced to an uncontested idealism. Theravāda is not equated without qualification with unchanged original Buddhism, and Pure Land is not classified as philosophically unserious popular faith.

Ethics is not forced into a single imported category. Intention, consequence, precept, virtue, vow, compassion, wisdom, karma, community, and liberation can all matter. The article also keeps institutional failures, hierarchy, gender, politics, colonialism, commodification, and modern scandals visible rather than treating Buddhist identity as a guarantee of peaceful or accountable conduct.

Comparisons with Jain, Brahmanical, Confucian, Daoist, Stoic, phenomenological, psychological, and scientific positions identify both resemblance and difference. The text avoids describing Chan as Daoism in Buddhist dress, emptiness as Advaita nonduality, not-self as Hume’s bundle theory, or meditation as ancient neuroscience.
