## Sources consulted

### Reference and specialist sources

- [SEP: Immanuel Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/) — the critical project’s historical setting and connections among cognition, metaphysics, freedom, morality, aesthetics, teleology, religion, and politics.
- [SEP: Kant’s Transcendental Idealism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-transcendental-idealism/) — empirical realism, appearances and things in themselves, and why familiar “one-world” and “two-world” labels do not exhaust the interpretive options.
- [SEP: Kant’s Account of Reason](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-reason/) — reason’s demand for completeness, dialectical illusion, and the constitutive/regulative distinction.
- [SEP: Kant’s Moral Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/) — good will, duty, hypothetical and categorical imperatives, the formulas of the moral law, autonomy, virtue, motivation, and the role of foreseeable consequences.
- [SEP: Kant’s Aesthetics and Teleology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-aesthetics/) — disinterested pleasure, universal validity without a determinate concept, the sublime, reflective judgment, purposiveness, and organisms.
- [SEP: Kant’s Social and Political Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-social-political/) — right, republicanism, public reason, history, cosmopolitanism, peace, property, punishment, and political limitations.
- [SEP: Kant’s Philosophy of Religion](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-religion/) — radical evil, ethical community, rational faith, and the limits of theoretical proof.
- [SEP: Philosophy of Race](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/race/) — the historical relation between Kant’s racial classifications and later scientific racism, with attention to changing scholarship and terminology.
- [IEP: Kant: Philosophical Development](https://iep.utm.edu/kantview/), [IEP: Kant’s Metaphysics](https://iep.utm.edu/kantmeta/), and [IEP: Kant’s Aesthetics](https://iep.utm.edu/kantaest/) — cross-checks on chronology, the critical turn, metaphysical limits, and the third Critique.

## Structure and editorial decisions

The former profile moved through the critical turn, Hume, appearances, ethics, judgment, and influence in six broad sections. The replacement uses seventeen ordered sections:

1. Königsberg, Prussia, and an Enlightenment in dispute
2. One critical project, three Critiques
3. The “Copernican revolution” and synthetic a priori judgment
4. Space, time, categories, and objective experience
5. Transcendental idealism without an unreal external world
6. Skepticism, metaphysical illusion, and regulative reason
7. Nature, causality, and the practical standpoint of freedom
8. Good will, duty, and kinds of imperative
9. The categorical imperative as a family of formulations
10. Virtue, character, motivation, and consequences
11. Happiness, the highest good, and religion
12. Beauty and the sublime
13. Organisms, purposiveness, and reflective judgment
14. Enlightenment, right, history, and cosmopolitan peace
15. Universalism beside exclusionary anthropology
16. Post-Kantian transformations and later inheritances
17. Live disputes and a practical reading route

Corrections and cautions include:

- Kant’s “Copernican revolution” is described as a methodological reversal about conditions of possible cognition, not the slogan that the mind invents reality.
- The article separates sensible intuition, space and time, concepts, categories, synthesis, judgment, and apperception instead of folding them into “mental structure.”
- Transcendental idealism is paired with empirical realism and does not imply that ordinary external objects are unreal; the thing-in-itself dispute remains open.
- Kant’s response to Hume concerns the conditions of objective temporal and causal experience, not a compromise or synthesis that simply takes content from empiricism and form from rationalism.
- The regulative use of reason is distinguished from constitutive knowledge, and practical faith is not represented as theoretical proof.
- The formulas of the categorical imperative are presented as a disputed family of formulations of one law, not as interchangeable slogans whose equivalence is assumed.
- Kantian ethics is not described as ignoring consequences: moral rightness is not grounded in aggregate outcomes, while agents must still reason about foreseeable effects, maxims, duties, and judgment.
- Aesthetics, teleology, religion, political right, history, and cosmopolitanism are treated as components of the critical project rather than afterthoughts.
- Kant’s racist hierarchy, gender hierarchy, and ambiguous relation to colonialism are integrated into the intellectual portrait. The article neither erases them nor treats universal language as an automatic acquittal.
- Kant is identified as the precursor and threshold for post-Kantian German Idealism, not simply as a completed German Idealist system-builder.

## Source limitations and rejected material

Wikipedia and general search-result snippets were used only for source discovery and were rejected as evidence for the draft and as visitor-facing Kant bibliography entries. No generic biography, unsourced quotation collection, AI-generated summary, or inaccessible page was treated as substantive support. Publisher pages and abstracts for paywalled books were used only to identify the documented scholarly dispute; no inaccessible argument was paraphrased as though its full text had been consulted. No exact quotation, page number, image source, or license was added.

The profile deliberately leaves several issues open for later claim review: the precise status of nonconceptual content; relations among the categorical imperative’s formulas; one-aspect/two-aspect and identity/nonidentity interpretations of appearances; changes across Kant’s racial and colonial writings; the relation of public right to private ethical virtue; and how far later appropriations remain Kantian. A future claim review should attach sentence-level sources and stable locators before changing the profile from `bibliography-only`.
