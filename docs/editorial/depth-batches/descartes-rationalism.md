### Rationalism, primary texts, and historiography

- The current SEP entry on [Early Modern Rationalism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-early-modern/) was consulted as a deliberately instrumental treatment of a fragmenting historiographical category. Its discussion of Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Amo, Cavendish, Conway, Sor Juana, and Poulain helped prevent the traditional triad from being treated as an exhaustive or self-declared party.
- The SEP entry on [Rationalism versus Empiricism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/) supplied distinctions among intuition/deduction, innate knowledge, innate concepts, and domain-specific forms of a priori commitment.
- Primary works by [Descartes](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/authors/descartes), [Spinoza](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/authors/spinoza), and [Leibniz](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/authors/leibniz) were consulted through Early Modern Texts and treated as rival systems, not chapters of one program.
- Alberto Vanzo’s [“Empiricism and Rationalism in Nineteenth-Century Histories of Philosophy”](https://philpapers.org/archive/VANEAR-2.pdf) informed the account of how the two triads became standard in Anglophone histories and curricula around the turn of the twentieth century.
- Peter Anstey and Alberto Vanzo’s [*Experimental Philosophy and the Origins of Empiricism*](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009030236) supplied the alternative experimental/speculative map and a warning against making the later binary the only structure of the period.
- SEP entries on [Epistemology in Classical Indian Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-india/) and [Epistemology in Chinese Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-epistemology/) guided limited, reversible comparisons. Nyāya, Buddhist epistemology, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, Mohism, Confucianism, Daoism, Zhu Xi, and Wang Yangming are not relabeled as rationalist or empiricist traditions.

Search-result snippets, generic summaries, quotation sites, videos, and AI-generated material were not treated as evidence or prose sources. Inaccessible chapters were used only through publisher metadata, contents, or accessible abstracts and are not represented as fully read.

## Editorial decisions and corrections

Descartes is no longer organized as an extended cogito-and-dualism summary. Method now includes intuition, deduction, analysis, review, hypotheses, observation, and experiment. Doubt is explicitly temporary and distinct from practical paralysis. The cogito is not presented as a settled syllogism or as immediately proving a complete immortal soul. The article distinguishes causal, preservation, and ontological arguments for God and leaves the Cartesian Circle open among competing reconstructions.

Ideas are treated as representational modes with contested ontological interpretation, and innateness includes powers and dispositions rather than a warehouse of conscious propositions. The error section connects finite intellect, wider will, suspension, freedom, and responsibility. The sensory and science sections correct the inaccurate picture in which a rationalist derives every physical fact a priori. The real distinction is paired with the experienced union; Elisabeth’s objection is treated as independent philosophy and not as a misunderstanding that Descartes straightforwardly solved.

Physics receives full treatment of extension, plenum, law, vortices, hypotheses, failure, and historical influence. Physiology and animals are separated so the animal-machine thesis can be described without claiming a false consensus about consciousness. The passions are naturally useful embodied orientations, not irrational intrusions to be crushed. Provisional morality, virtue, generosity, limited information, and Elisabeth’s ethical objections make the practical philosophy part of the system rather than an appendix.

Rationalism is defined as a contested retrospective family of domain-sensitive commitments. It is not “reason instead of experience,” one continental manifesto, or an essence shared by three men. The article distinguishes intuition/deduction, innate knowledge, innate concepts, a priori warrant, certainty, modality, sufficient reason, and intelligibility. Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz receive separate treatments because their ontologies and accounts of freedom are incompatible.

Scholastic and scientific contexts precede the canonical triad. Elisabeth, Conway, Cavendish, Amo, Du Châtelet, Poulain, and Sor Juana broaden the map without being forced into one identity. Experience receives its own section inside rationalist inquiry. Empiricism is presented as another retrospective family and as a set of argumentative burdens rather than an opposing nation. Kant transforms the problem while also helping construct its later historiography. Later idealism, logic, logical empiricism, Quine, Popper, cognitive science, machine learning, and explainable systems are treated as selective inheritances, not one continuous rationalist school.

Indian and Chinese comparisons are explicitly local, problem-centered, and reversible. Similar concerns about perception, inference, testimony, necessity, principle, or moral knowledge do not authorize relabeling Nyāya, Buddhist epistemology, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, Mohism, Confucianism, or Daoism as early versions of a European category.

## Source limitations and later claim review

This batch is an editorially responsible synthesis, not a claim review or a verdict on the major disputes. Later source mapping should use stable primary locators for the method rules; each stage of doubt; the cogito; idea taxonomy; the causal, preservation, and ontological arguments; clear and distinct perception; the Circle; error and freedom; external-world reconstruction; real distinction and union; Elisabeth’s objections and Descartes’s replies; laws and particular physical hypotheses; animal cognition; passion; generosity; and reception claims.

Rationalism source mapping should separately cover the historiography of the category; scholastic contexts; each criterion proposed for rationalism; Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz; the expanded early modern canon; Locke and Hume; Kant; later transformations; contemporary a priori and nativist disputes; and every cross-cultural comparison. Until that work is completed by the recorded editorial process, both pages properly remain `bibliography-only`.
