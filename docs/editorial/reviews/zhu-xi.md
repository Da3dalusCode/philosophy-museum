## Corrections and qualifications

- “Neo-Confucianism” is identified as a modern English umbrella. `Daoxue`, `lixue`, and Cheng–Zhu are historically situated and partly retrospective classifications, not one timeless self-declared denomination.
- `Li` is pattern, order, coherence, or principle and is not automatically a separately existing Platonic Form. `Qi` is psychophysical stuff or vital material force, not inert matter. They are conceptually distinguishable but inseparable in concrete things.
- Human nature’s goodness does not make embodied emotions or ordinary desires intrinsically evil. The ethical issue is fitting response versus selfish distortion.
- Zhu Xi selected, edited, ordered, and commented on inherited texts as the Four Books. He did not author them or discover an uncontested ancient package.
- `Gewu` includes inquiry into texts, events, natural phenomena, relationships, and practical affairs. It is neither identical to modern laboratory science nor adequately represented by the later bamboo anecdote.
- Zhu distinguishes knowing from acting, gives knowledge guiding priority, and gives practice completing weight. He does not defend inert memorization.
- Quiet-sitting may collect attention, but `jing` is an alert orientation meant to operate within ordinary duties rather than Buddhist withdrawal.
- The profile reports Zhu’s criticisms of Buddhism and Daoism as polemical classifications, not neutral descriptions of internally diverse traditions.
- Zhu Xi was condemned and restricted near the end of his life. Yuan privileging of his Four Books commentaries in 1313 was posthumous, and later Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese receptions were not uniform.

## Translation, authority, and unresolved questions

No translated primary passage is quoted as though one modern English wording were neutral. Unresolved questions include the best controlled translation of `li` and `qi`; the precise balance of gradual accumulation and breakthrough in investigation; whether Zhu’s safeguards against self-centered desire adequately challenge inherited hierarchy; how Buddhist and Daoist exchange should be distinguished from polemical boundary-making; and when common education becomes constraining orthodoxy.

The article treats canon formation as both philosophical pedagogy and institutional power. This preserves the achievement of a disciplined common curriculum without turning later examination authority into proof of philosophical correctness or projecting that authority backward into Zhu Xi’s lifetime.
