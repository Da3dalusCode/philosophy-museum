## Editorial decision

The article’s overall selection is good: it treats univocity, formal distinction, individuation, freedom, cognition, natural theology, and textual method. Its chief weakness is a comparative story in which Aquinas supplies “the synthesis,” Scotus revises it, and Ockham radicalizes Scotus. That textbook lineage obscures Scotus’s Franciscan, Avicennian, Augustinian, Aristotelian, Anselmian, and multi-version textual settings. The revision should retain all 13 sections and remain close to the current length while replacing the crude voluntarist and post-Aquinas framing.

The governing interpretation is: Scotus develops a metaphysics of being, common nature and individuation; an epistemology of abstraction, intuitive cognition, and anti-skepticism; a natural theology of infinite being; and a moral psychology of synchronic contingency and freedom intrinsic to will. Univocity is conceptual/semantic and demonstrative, not the claim that God and creatures form one genus or exist in the same mode. Formal distinction and haecceity cannot become mysterious labels. His ethics cannot be summarized as “God can command anything.”

### Exact replacement copy for the highest-risk passages

**`univocity`, both paragraphs**

> Scotus argues that at least some concepts must be univocally predicable of God and creatures: the concept remains stable enough that affirming it and denying it of the same subject would be a contradiction. Because human cognition begins from sensible creatures, such conceptual stability is necessary if demonstrations from effects are to conclude anything about God without equivocation. *Ordinatio* I, distinction 3 develops several arguments for this thesis, including the possibility of being certain that something is a being while remaining uncertain whether it is finite or infinite.
>
> Univocity does not place God and creatures inside one genus or assign them the same mode of existence. “Infinite” and “finite” are intrinsic modes of being, and infinite being is not a quantitatively larger specimen. The thesis concerns concepts, predication, and demonstrative continuity; it coexists with an infinite difference in mode. Whether that distinction adequately protects transcendence is a real dispute, but calling Scotus’s view “God is one being among others” simply misses the claim.

**`will-freedom`, both paragraphs**

> Scotus locates freedom intrinsically in will. A free volition is synchronically contingent: at the very moment an agent wills one option, the will retains a real power for another. This is stronger than saying deliberation may present different goods at different times. Scotus’s discussions of the affection for advantage and affection for justice help distinguish willing a good for fulfillment from the will’s capacity to love according to justice, but his use of that Anselmian vocabulary changes across the *Lectura*, *Ordinatio*, and *Reportatio*.
>
> Divine and created freedom make contingency fundamental, but they do not yield a one-word “voluntarism.” Scotus treats some moral requirements as necessary while holding that many positive laws depend on God’s free ordination. Scholars dispute how wide that necessary core is and whether the strongest arbitrary-will interpretation survives the texts. The article should teach this dispute rather than announce either that morality is arbitrary or that the problem disappears.

**`works-method`, paragraph 1**

> Scotus’s corpus survives in different authorial and pedagogical states. The *Lectura* records his 1298–1299 Oxford lectures on the *Sentences*. He revised those materials into the *Ordinatio* from about 1300 until his death, leaving it unfinished. The Paris *Reportationes* are student reports whose chronology does not line up simply with every part of the *Ordinatio*. His corpus also includes Aristotle questions, *Collationes*, a quodlibetal disputation, the late *De primo principio*, and *De formalitatibus*. Earlier doubts surrounded works such as the *Theoremata*; its recent critical edition accepts it as genuine. Each claim should follow the relevant textual layer rather than treating “Scotus” as one polished book.
