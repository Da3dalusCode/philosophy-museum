## Editorial decision

The current article is already close to the correct model: scholasticism as a changing culture of textual study, questions, objections, replies, commentary, and disputation—not a doctrine. Its base branch record, however, still says scholastics use “structured argument to reconcile inherited authorities, reason, and theology,” asks only how reasoning clarifies faith, relates only logic and Philosophy of Religion, and names only Aquinas and Ockham. That base record recreates the exact unified/Thomistic caricature the article tries to defeat.

The revision should retain all nine article sections and remain only modestly above 2,000 words. “Medieval scholasticism” should be defined chiefly for Latin school, mendicant *studium*, court, and university practices while showing their changing connections to Greek, Arabic, Hebrew, and Byzantine texts and institutions. Islamic, Jewish, and Byzantine philosophies were autonomous and internally plural traditions, not early departments of “Latin scholasticism.” Translation was multilingual, multidirectional, selective, and transformative. The major period does not end neatly in 1500; later scholasticisms continued into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

### Exact replacement copy for the highest-risk passages

**`overview`, scope paragraph**

> Scholasticism is most precise when used for changing learned practices in Latin schools, mendicant houses, courts, and universities: close reading of authoritative texts, logical analysis, question-commentary, objections and replies, and formal disputation. These practices never produced one doctrine, and they did not encompass all Latin medieval thought. They also developed through contact with Greek, Arabic, Hebrew, and Byzantine texts and scholars. Islamic, Jewish, and Byzantine philosophies must remain autonomous traditions with their own institutions, genres, languages, and disputes rather than being renamed parts of Latin scholasticism.

**`historical-development`, translation paragraph**

> Medieval philosophical transmission followed several routes: Greek texts and commentaries into Arabic; Greek and Arabic works into Latin; Arabic works into Hebrew; Greek and Latin works into Hebrew; and Latin works into Greek, among others. Translators, patrons, physicians, jurists, theologians, and commentators selected vocabulary and reshaped arguments. Arabic-Latin translations of Aristotle, Avicenna, Averroes, al-Ghazali, and scientific works transformed thirteenth-century Latin curricula, while Arabic-Hebrew translation supported distinct Jewish philosophical developments. “Recovery of Aristotle” is therefore too passive and too narrow for a multilingual history of composition, translation, commentary, disagreement, and return.

**`modern-relevance`, method paragraph**

> Scholastic disputation made disagreement into a structured practice, but it was not an egalitarian modern seminar. Participants advanced objections and responses; reports could be revised into written questions; and a master concluded with a *determinatio*. The form could expose positions to unusually sustained testing while also controlling who participated, what authorities counted, and who determined the result. Its modern value lies in comparing institutional designs for disagreement—not in claiming that medieval universities invented neutral peer review or “adversarial charity.”

## Residual uncertainty

Keep visible: the porous boundary of “scholasticism”; uneven institutional development across cities and centuries; how far specific genres were performed versus later redacted; local differences in curriculum and censure; incomplete survival of translations and reports; and the conventional nature of 1050/1100 and 1500 period markers. The safe solution is explicit scope, not one synthesized “medieval method.”
