## Editorial decision

The current article rightly goes beyond the razor, but its governing vocabulary—“critic of inflated metaphysics,” “pare back,” “aggressively reductionist,” “fewer structures”—still invites the false picture of Ockham as anti-metaphysical proto-empiricist. Ockham argued extensively in metaphysics and natural philosophy. His distinctive combination is semantic analysis, a two-category created ontology of singular substances and qualities (with limited theological exceptions), a rejection of extra-mental universals, a changing account of universal concepts, intuitive/abstractive cognition, theology, virtue theory, and political argument.

The revision should keep all 14 sections and approximately the current length. Its mandatory corrections are: the celebrated razor wording is not Ockham’s; the fuller permission to posit includes reason/self-evident premises, experience, and infallible Scripture; he does **not** reject universals by the razor; supposition must use personal/simple/material accurately; abstractive cognition is defined partly by abstraction from existence/nonexistence, not abstraction to a universal; and the unfinished *Dialogus* presents a dialogue among positions that cannot automatically be attributed to its author.

### Exact replacement copy for the highest-risk passages

**`razor`, both paragraphs**

> “Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity” is the famous later formulation of Ockham’s razor, not a sentence found in Ockham’s writings. His own methodological rule is more constrained: one should not posit a plurality without necessity, where a posit may be warranted by valid reasoning from self-evident premises, experience, or infallible scriptural authority. Economy is therefore not an automatic preference for whichever theory looks shortest, and theological commitments remain among Ockham’s admitted grounds.
>
> The razor does not do every reductive job attributed to it. Ockham rejects extra-mental universals because he argues that the realist accounts available to him are incoherent or conflict with other commitments, not simply because universals add entities. He uses parsimony more directly in reducing ontological categories such as quantity and relation. Each application needs its own argument; the razor is a rule of warranted commitment, not a machine for generating truth.

**`mental-language`, paragraph 1**

> Ockham distinguishes written, spoken, and mental language. Written signs are conventionally subordinated to spoken signs, and spoken signs to naturally signifying concepts that combine into mental propositions. A term signifies wherever it occurs, but it supposits only within a proposition. In personal supposition a term stands for what it signifies, as “dog” does in “Every dog is an animal.” In simple supposition it stands for a concept, as in “Dog is a species.” In material supposition it stands for a spoken or written expression, as in “Dog has three letters.” These shifts help specify a proposition’s truth conditions; they are not merely wordplay.

**`knowledge-intuition`, paragraph 1**

> Ockham distinguishes intuitive from abstractive cognition by their relation to evident existential judgment. In normal circumstances, intuitive cognition of an object can ground evident judgment that it exists, or intuitive cognition of its absence that it does not. Abstractive cognition abstracts from existence and nonexistence: it can represent or reason about an object without grounding that existential judgment. This distinction is not the same as the contrast between sensing an individual and forming a universal concept, although it intersects with Ockham’s theory of concept formation.

**`politics-poverty`, paragraph 2**

> Ockham’s later writings argue within Christian legal and ecclesiological frameworks that papal power is limited, a pope may persist in heresy, and spiritual and temporal authorities do not collapse into one unlimited jurisdiction. These claims do not make him a modern liberal or advocate of a secular state. The unfinished *Dialogus* is especially delicate evidence because its speakers explore competing positions; a character’s argument is not automatically Ockham’s conclusion. Assertive works such as the *Work of Ninety Days* and *Short Discourse on Tyrannical Government* must anchor claims about his own position.
