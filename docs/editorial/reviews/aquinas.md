## Editorial decision

The current article has a sound architecture and clears the hard depth floor, but it is not ready for claim review in its present form. It repeatedly compresses a contested, textually varied project into textbook formulas: “integration” of Aristotle and Christianity, essence versus existence in all “created things,” five self-contained proofs, a generic list of natural-law goods, a settled account of freedom, and a single “doctrine of analogy.” The revision should preserve the 13-section structure and roughly the current length. It should deepen by replacement, not by indiscriminate expansion.

The governing interpretation is: Aquinas is a Dominican theologian and philosopher in the Latin scholastic world whose philosophical arguments are inseparable from, but not reducible to, *sacra doctrina*. His work transforms Aristotelian, Augustinian, Neoplatonic, Avicennian, Averroist, Maimonidean, patristic, biblical, juridical, and university materials. The *Summa theologiae* is one pedagogically ordered and unfinished synthesis inside a disputed century, not the medieval settlement.

### Exact replacement copy for the highest-risk passages

Use or closely preserve the following language during canonical integration.

**`being-metaphysics`, paragraph 1**

> In *On Being and Essence* and related works, Aquinas asks how a created substance’s essence—what it is—relates to its act of being. Created substances do not exist through their essences alone: their being is received and limited according to what they are. God, by contrast, is not one participant in being but subsistent being itself. Interpreters disagree over how best to describe the essence–existence composition and over how uniformly it applies across Aquinas’s corpus. The distinction should therefore explain created dependence without turning “existence” into a separable object added to every thing.

**`five-ways`, both paragraphs**

> The Five Ways occupy one article near the beginning of the *Summa theologiae*’s treatment of God. Aquinas first argues that God’s existence is not self-evident to us but may be demonstrated from effects better known to us. The five compressed routes then begin from change, ordered efficient causation, possibility and necessity, gradations of being and goodness, and goal-directed natural activity. They presuppose Aristotelian causal and modal vocabulary; they are neither experiments in physics nor appeals to whatever science has not yet explained.
>
> The Ways do not by themselves establish every attribute of the Christian God. Aquinas’s subsequent questions argue from a first source to simplicity, perfection, goodness, knowledge, will, and other divine predicates. The first two Ways especially concern causal series whose present derivative activity requires a first cause, not merely a first event in a distant temporal sequence. The Third and Fourth Ways remain especially disputed, and the Fifth concerns regular end-directed natural activity rather than biological complexity alone. A responsible presentation shows the arguments’ larger textual setting and leaves their validity open for assessment.

**`ethics-law-politics`, paragraph 1**

> Aquinas’s ethics joins action toward perceived goods, habits and virtues, law, grace, and beatitude. Natural law is the rational creature’s participation in eternal law, and its first practical principle directs agents to pursue good and avoid evil. Aquinas orders further precepts through inclinations to preserve life; to sexual union, offspring, and their care; and, as rational beings, to know truth about God and live socially. These are not a modern checklist of self-evident policies. Secondary conclusions can fail in particular cases or be obscured by bad custom, so practical reasoning and virtue remain indispensable.

**`analogy-divine-language`, paragraph 1**

> Aquinas approaches divine language through several connected distinctions rather than one detachable formula called “the doctrine of analogy.” Human names arise from knowledge of creatures and therefore do not express God’s essence as it is in itself. Yet names such as good and wise can signify perfections substantially of God. They are not predicated of God and creatures in exactly the same mode, but neither are they wholly unrelated equivocations. Analogy preserves meaningful predication from effects while marking the disproportion between finite creatures and their cause.
